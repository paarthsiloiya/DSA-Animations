List Rotation

Rotating a linked list means moving its starting point: after rotating a list left by k positions, the node that was k steps after the head becomes the new head, and the first k nodes reappear at the end, in order. The list keeps all of its nodes — only the head pointer (and the tail's link) changes.

How It Works

The most direct way to rotate left by one is to detach the current head and reattach it at the tail: remember the old head, let the second node become the new head, walk to the last node, set the old head's next to None, and make the tail point to the old head. Repeating that k times rotates the list by k — exactly what the animation below does.

The standard optimization walks to the tail only once: close the list into a ring by pointing the tail at the head, advance the head pointer k times (reducing k modulo n first), then cut the ring open just before the new head.

Worked Example — Rotating 5 -> 2 -> 6 -> 3 -> 1 by k = 2

The animation arranges the five nodes on an arc and performs k = 2 single-step rotations:

  • Rotation 1: the node 5 is marked as the Head and 2 as the New Head. A cursor then walks from the head to the tail — that walk is the while tail.next is not None loop. The head node 5 is detached (its outgoing arrow is removed, its next resets to NULL), and it is reattached after the tail with a fresh arrow. The list is now 2 → 6 → 3 → 1 → 5.
  • Rotation 2: the same procedure detaches 2 and reattaches it at the end, producing 6 → 3 → 1 → 5 → 2 — the original list rotated left by two positions, which the final row arrangement shows.

Note what the animation repeats on every rotation: the walk to the tail. That walk is what makes the repeated-single-step method cost Θ(n) per rotation.

Complexity

  • As animated (k single-step rotations): each rotation walks the list to find the tail, so the total is $\Theta(k \cdot n)$ time — Best case $O(k)$ when the list is so short the walk is trivial, Worst and Average case $\Theta(k \cdot n)$ since every rotation traverses up to all $n$ nodes.
  • Standard efficient method: one $\Theta(n)$ pass to find the tail and close the ring, then k mod n pointer hops — $\Theta(n)$ time overall, which is $O(n + k)$ reduced by the modulo; every node is reached at most once.
  • Space: $O(1)$ — only a constant number of pointers is needed.

Pitfall — k > n:

  • Rotating by k = n returns the original list, so any k must first be reduced with k = k mod n. Without the modulo, the repeated-step method wastes full cycles of work, and the ring-advance method hops past the tail and breaks the list.
  • Edge cases: an empty list or a single-node list is its own rotation — always check head is not None before touching pointers.

Must-Know Points

  • Rotation reorders nothing inside memory — only next pointers (and the head) are rewritten.
  • A singly linked list can rotate left cheaply (detach head, append to tail) but rotating right requires walking to the tail anyway — a doubly linked list avoids that walk.
  • Rotation is the engine behind circular buffers, round-robin scheduling, and the "rotate array" interview problem — on arrays it is done with three reversals instead of pointer moves.
