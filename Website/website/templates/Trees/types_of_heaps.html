Types of Heaps

A heap is a complete binary tree with one ordering rule between neighbours: in a max-heap every parent is ≥ its children (the maximum sits at the root); in a min-heap every parent is ≤ its children (the minimum sits at the root). That makes a heap the canonical structure for priority queues — the extreme element is always one glance away, at the root.

The Two Defining Properties

  • Shape property: a heap is a complete binary tree — every level full except possibly the last, which fills strictly left to right. No gaps, ever.
  • Heap property: only parents and children are ordered — siblings and cousins are not. (Contrast a BST, where all values are ordered across subtrees.)

The shape property is why heaps live happily in a plain array (the list representation): with no gaps, node i's children are at 2i + 1 and 2i + 2, and its parent at ⌊(i − 1)/2⌋ — exactly the formulas the insertion code below uses. Both animations below draw the tree form while maintaining the array underneath.

Max-Heap — Insertion with Sift-Up

A new value joins at the next free slot (keeping the tree complete), then sifts up: while it beats its parent, the two swap. The animation inserts 2, 7, 26, 25, 19, 17, 1, 90, 3, 36 into an empty heap.

Worked example — the swaps the animation performs:

  • 2 becomes the root; 7 > 2 — one swap, 7 is the new root.
  • 26 > 7 — one swap, 26 takes the root; 25 swaps past 2 and settles under 26 (25 ≤ 26).
  • 19 and 1 sit still (19 ≤ 25, 1 ≤ 2); 17 > 7 — one swap up beside 26.
  • The dramatic one: 90 — three swaps (past 2, past 25, past 26) bubble it all the way to the root.
  • 3 sits still (3 ≤ 26); 36 > 25 and then 36 > 26 — two swaps, settling under 90.

The final array reads 90 36 17 25 26 7 1 2 3 19 — check it: every parent dominates its children. Each insert's "swapping" and "correct position" captions in the video are exactly the two branches of the sift-up loop.

Complexity: the sift-up path has at most one node per level — $O(\log n)$ worst case (the recurrence is $T(n) = T(n/2) + O(1)$; the height is $\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor$). Best case $O(1)$: the newcomer already obeys the heap property (like 19, 1 and 3 above). Average over random inputs is $O(1)$ — a new value usually stops after a couple of swaps — with $O(\log n)$ the tight worst-case bound. Space: $O(1)$ extra (the array is the heap; no recursion needed).

Min-Heap — the Mirror Image

A min-heap flips one comparison: the newcomer swaps up while it is smaller than its parent, so the minimum ends at the root. The animation inserts 26, 7, 2, 25, 19, 17, 1, 90, 36, 3.

What the animation shows: 26 starts as the root; 7 and 2 each take one swap to reach the top (2 ends as the root — the smallest so far); 25, 19 and 17 slot in with at most one swap; then 1 bubbles two levels (past 19, past 2) to become the root; and 3 finishes with two swaps (past 36’s slot value 25, then past 19). The final array is 1 3 2 26 19 17 7 90 36 25 — the minimum, 1, sits at index 0, and every parent is ≤ its children. Same loop, same $O(\log n)$ worst case — only the comparison flipped.

Must-Know Points

  • Only the extreme element is cheap: the root is the min/max in $O(1)$, but an arbitrary search must scan — a heap does not order across subtrees the way a BST does.
  • Insert at the end, sift up (shown here); the mirror operation — remove the root, move the last element to the top, sift down — is what Heap Sort and priority queues use for extraction. (Deletion is a natural future section for this page; these animations cover insertion.)
  • Heaps are not sorted arrays: the array form satisfies the heap property level by level — 90 36 17 25 26 … is a valid heap and not sorted.
  • Max or min is one comparison: negating the comparison converts one into the other — the code tabs differ in exactly that operator.
