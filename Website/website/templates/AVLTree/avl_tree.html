AVL Trees

An AVL tree (Adelson-Velsky and Landis, 1962) is a self-balancing binary search tree. A plain BST can degenerate into a chain — insert sorted data and every operation collapses from $O(\log n)$ to $O(n)$. The AVL tree prevents that by keeping one invariant on every node: the balance factor

$\text{balance}(v) = \text{height}(v.\text{left}) - \text{height}(v.\text{right})$

must satisfy $|\text{balance}(v)| \le 1$. Whenever an insertion or deletion pushes some node's balance factor to $\pm 2$, a rotation — a constant-number-of-pointer change that re-roots a small subtree — restores the invariant. The pages below animate all six moments: two full operations and the four imbalance shapes.

Insertion

Insertion first walks the plain BST path (smaller → left, larger → right) and places the new node. On the way back up, every node on the path recomputes its height and balance factor (each node's display shows its live balance factor in the animation). The first node whose factor reaches $\pm 2$ is fixed with the matching rotation, and — the AVL theorem — one rebalance is always enough: fixing the lowest imbalanced node restores the whole tree.

Worked example — the animation inserts 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 2, 1, 9, 8, 10, 11:

  • Inserting 3, 4 is plain BST work; 5 pushes node 3's balance to −2 (right-heavy) — a single left rotation makes 4 the root.
  • 6 settles; 7 pushes node 5 to −2 — another left rotation lifts 6.
  • 2 settles; 1 pushes node 3 to +2 (left-heavy) — a single right rotation lifts 2 above 3.
  • The double case: 8 slides under 9's left (9 is the right child of the root 7), making 7's factor −2 and the offending grandchild on the inner side — the Right-Left shape: first a right rotation on child 9, then a left rotation on 7.
  • 10 triggers one left rotation on 6; 11 one more on 9. The final tree reads level by level as 4 2 8 1 3 6 10 5 7 9 11 with height 4 — an 11-node tree kept at $\lfloor \log_2 11 \rfloor + 1$ levels.

Complexity: the descent is one $O(\log n)$ BST walk; the upward pass does $O(1)$ work per level (height + balance update) and stops at the first rotation. Worst case $O(\log n)$ — provable because the AVL invariant bounds the height at $h \le 1.44 \cdot \log_2(n+2)$ (the standard AVL bound: the worst AVL shape is a Fibonacci-tree, whose size grows by $\varphi \approx 1.618$ per level, giving $\log_\varphi 2 \approx 1.44$). Space $O(\log n)$ recursion depth.

Deletion — Rebalance on the Way Up

Deletion is the standard BST four-case procedure. The AVL difference comes afterwards: as the recursion returns, every node recomputes its balance, and any node that hits $\pm 2$ is rotated — using the child's balance factor to pick the case. Unlike insertion, one deletion can trigger $O(\log n)$ rotations (the rebalance can propagate all the way to the root), but each is $O(1)$ and the total stays $O(\log n)$.

What the animation shows: the AVL tree built from 26, 7, 2, 25, 19, 47, 1, 90, 36, 3, 34 fades in (its root is 25), then four deletions: 1 (a leaf — removed, no rotation needed), 25 (root, two children — replaced by its in-order successor 26), 26 (again the root, two children — replaced by successor 34), and finally 19 — whose removal leaves node 7 at +2 with an inner (right) heavy left child: the Left-Right case, announced in the animation as "Left Rotation Required" then "Right Rotation Required". The child rule at work: node 7's left child had balance < 0, so the left child rotates first.

Left-Left Case — Single Right Rotation

Shape: a node goes +2 and the deep growth is on the outer left path — the new arrival descended left, then left again from the imbalanced node (in the animation: 5 → 4 → 3). Fix: one right rotation — exactly what the video's red "Right Rotation" label says: the left child (4) lifts to the top, the old node (5) drops to its right, and the middle subtree (Y) is handed over. All three balance factors end at 0.

Animation trace: insert 5, hang X off its right, insert 4 (5's balance = +1), then insert 3 under 4's left — node 5 hits +2, "IMBALANCED!". Placeholder subtrees (Z, W under 3, Y under 4's right) make the shape general. The rotation lifts 4 to the root: 3 keeps Z and W beneath it, Y hops across to become 5's left, and 5 settles as 4's right child — every balance factor back to 0, "Tree is now balanced!"

Right-Right Case — Single Left Rotation

Shape: the mirror — a node goes −2 and the growth is on the outer right path (3 → 4 → 5). Fix: one left rotation, as the video's "Left Rotation" label says: the right child (4) lifts, the old node (3) drops to its left, and the Y subtree crosses over.

Animation trace: insert 3, hang X off its left, insert 4 (3's balance = −1), insert 5 under 4 — node 3 hits −2, "IMBALANCED!". The left rotation lifts 4: 3 drops to 4's left, Y becomes 3's right, 5 keeps Z and W — all balances 0, "Tree is now balanced!"

Left-Right Case — Left Rotation, Then Right

Shape: the node goes +2 but the growth is on the inner path — left child, then right (5 → 3 → 4). A single rotation cannot fix a zig-zag, so it is straightened first: step 1 left-rotates the left child (turning the zig-zag into a Left-Left shape), step 2 right-rotates the node. The video labels the steps exactly: "Step 1: Left rotation (3-4)" then "Step 2: Right rotation (5-4)".

Animation trace: insert 5, X on its right, 3 on its left (+1), Y under 3's left, then insert 4 under 3's right — node 5 hits +2, "IMBALANCED!". Step 1 rotates 3-4: 4 lifts above 3, Z moves to 3's right. Now 5–4–3 is a clean Left-Left chain, and step 2's right rotation lifts 4 to the root: 3 (with Y, Z) on its left, 5 (with W, X) on its right — balanced, all factors 0.

Right-Left Case — Right Rotation, Then Left

Shape: the mirror zig-zag — node at −2, growth on the inner right path: right child, then left (3 → 5 → 4). Step 1 right-rotates the right child (5-4) to straighten the kink into a Right-Right chain, step 2 left-rotates the node (3-4). The video's labels: "Step 1: Right rotation (5-4)", "Step 2: Left rotation (3-4)".

Animation trace: insert 3, X on its left, 5 on its right (−1), Y under 5's right, then insert 4 under 5's left — node 3 hits −2, "IMBALANCED!". Step 1 rotates 5-4 (4 lifts, W crosses to 5); step 2's left rotation lifts 4 to the root: 3 (with X, Z) on its left, 5 (with W, Y) on its right — every factor 0, "Tree is now balanced!"

Complexity Summary

  • Search / Insert / Delete: $O(\log n)$ worst case — the height bound $h \le 1.44 \log_2 n$ caps every root-to-leaf path; the BST-style descent is $O(h)$, and insert adds one rotation, delete at most $O(h)$ rotations of $O(1)$ each.
  • Rotations: always $O(1)$ — a fixed number of pointer rewrites plus two height recomputations, as the code tabs show.
  • Best case for insert with no rebalance: the plain $O(\log n)$ walk with zero rotations (most insertions rotate nothing).
  • Space: $\Theta(n)$ nodes, each storing key, two pointers and one height — plus $O(\log n)$ recursion depth per operation.

Must know: the four cases are named by where the growth happened (LL/LR/RL/RR), and each is fixed by the rotation(s) named after the shape it produces: LL needs a right rotation, RR a left, LR is left-then-right, RL is right-then-left — precisely the four videos above. Compared with a plain BST, AVL keeps the $O(\log n)$ guarantee that sorted input destroys; compared with heaps, it retains full ordered search rather than only find-min/max.
