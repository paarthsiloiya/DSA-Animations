Median of Medians

Median of medians answers one question: how do you pick a provably decent pivot for selection, every single time? Quickselect is blazing fast on average, but a stream of unlucky (or adversarial) pivots degrades it to $O(n^2)$. Median-of-medians replaces the lucky guess with a pivot that is guaranteed to sit between the 30th and 70th percentiles of the data — which is exactly what selection needs to run in worst-case $O(n)$.

Groups of Five, Recursively

  1. Split the $n$ values into groups of 5 (the last may be short).
  2. Sort each group and take its middle element — the group's median.
  3. Recurse on the medians themselves: their median is the pivot.

Why must that pivot be decent? The pivot is the median of roughly $n/5$ group medians, so at least half the group medians are $\le$ pivot (and half $\ge$). Each such group median exceeds 3 of its own group's 5 elements (it sits in the middle). Multiply: about $3 \cdot n/10$ elements are guaranteed below the pivot, and symmetrically above. Discard the wrong side and you have thrown away at least $3n/10$ elements — a 30% haircut, guaranteed, every level.

What the animation shows: 50 values (with one duplicate, 94). Frames snap around the groups of 5; each group collapses into a sorted column; the middle of each column lights up as its median and everything else fades. The 10 surviving medians — [45, 56, 63, 52, 33, 61, 57, 66, 48, 58] — get regrouped, resorted, and re-medianed, leaving 52 and 58; the tiny final group's middle (its second element) is marked as the result: 58.

Honest framing: 58 is not the exact median of the 50 values (that honour falls to the 25th–26th sorted values, 56 and 57). It doesn't have to be — median-of-medians is a pivot selector. The verified scoreboard: 26 values below 58, 23 above — comfortably inside the guaranteed 30–70% band, bought for a fraction of the work a true median would cost.

Complexity

  • Best / Worst / Average: $O(n)$ — the recursion is uneven on purpose: $$T(n) = T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n)$$ One call finds the pivot's rank among the medians ($T(n/5)$); the other is the selection itself, which after the 30% haircut recurses on at most $7n/10$ elements. The master theorem does not apply (the subproblems are unequal), but the substitution proof is short: assume $T(k) \le ak$ for $k < n$; then $$T(n) \le a\tfrac{n}{5} + a\tfrac{7n}{10} + cn = 0.9\,an + cn \le an$$ for any $a \ge 10c$. The two fractions must sum below 1 — which is exactly why the groups have 5 elements: groups of 3 give $n/3 + 2n/3 = n$ (dead even, no progress) and the argument collapses.
  • Space: $O(n)$ as shown (the group copies and median list), with $\log$-shaped recursion depth along the $n/5$ pivot chain; in-place variants trim the extra space to $O(1)$ plus the stack.

Must know: this algorithm selects a pivot — full selection (finding the $k$-th smallest) is this + one partition + one recursive call, the introselect structure; the constant is famously large, so practical libraries use quickselect and only fall back to median-of-medians when quickselect misbehaves; and the 30%-guarantee is a rank guarantee on the pivot, not a promise of the exact median.

Applications

  • Guaranteed selection: the $k$-th smallest / median in worst-case linear time (introselect).
  • Robust statistics: the true median resists outliers that poison the mean.
  • Quicksort hardening: adversarial-input-proof pivot choice.
  • Percentile queries: steady worst-case behaviour for order statistics at scale.

Strategy Average Worst case
Sort, then index $O(n \log n)$ $O(n \log n)$
